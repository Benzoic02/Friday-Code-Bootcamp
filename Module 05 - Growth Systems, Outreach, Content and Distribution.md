# Module 05: Find Your First Users

## The goal: find and talk to users

A product does not grow just because it exists. People need a reason to notice, trust, try, and return. Friday can help research leads, draft messages, turn product knowledge into content, create assets, and organize recurring work. You decide who to contact, what to promise, when to publish, and whether a message should be sent.

Growth is not a synonym for sending more messages. A sustainable system starts with a clear audience and a useful offer. It respects consent, privacy, platform rules, and the recipient's time. It also measures whether conversations and content produce learning or qualified interest, rather than celebrating volume alone.

By the end of this module, you will have a small repeatable outreach workflow, a content plan tied to real product evidence, and a practical exercise to run with a limited audience. You can then decide whether any step is safe and valuable enough to automate.

## Start with a specific audience and promise

“Anyone who wants to be more productive” is not a usable target. Narrow the audience by role, situation, and problem. For example: independent bookkeepers who spend time chasing missing client receipts, or volunteer coordinators who schedule recurring shifts with spreadsheets. A specific group makes it easier to find relevant public information and write content that sounds useful rather than generic.

Describe your offer in one plain sentence: “This helps [specific person] do [job] with [concrete benefit], without [common frustration].” Avoid claims you cannot prove. If you have not measured time saved, do not claim that the product saves 10 hours per week. Use a testable statement such as “Try a faster way to collect approvals” until you have real evidence.

Choose a single growth goal for the next two weeks. It could be five qualified interviews, three pilot users, ten relevant waitlist signups, or clear evidence that a particular message is not resonating. Define “qualified” before collecting names. For instance, a qualified lead might match the audience, experience the problem, and have authority to test a solution.

## Build a considerate outreach pipeline

A simple pipeline can be represented as stages: discover a potential fit, confirm relevance, research the person's public professional context, draft a message, review it, send only through an appropriate channel, record the outcome, and honor any request not to follow up. Friday may help with research, organization, and drafting when you have the right tools connected. Integration capabilities and exact controls vary, so verify available behavior before relying on it.

### Step 1: Identify a legitimate source

Use public professional directories, opt-in communities, referrals, event participant lists where follow-up is appropriate, or people who have invited contact. Do not harvest private addresses, evade access controls, or build a database from personal data without a lawful basis and a clear need. The fact that information is visible on a website does not automatically make bulk collection or reuse appropriate.

Start with a small list, perhaps 10 to 20 people. The purpose is to learn whether your targeting and message make sense, not to maximize the number of recipients. Record where each contact came from and why the person appears relevant. If you cannot explain the fit in one sentence, do more research or do not contact them.

### Step 2: Research the recipient, not just the company

Look for a relevant, public professional signal: a role, a product or service they describe, a recent article, or a stated operational challenge. Keep research factual and modest. Do not infer sensitive traits, family details, health, or personal circumstances. Avoid referencing information that would feel invasive even if it is technically public.

Ask Friday to summarize only facts from supplied, permitted public sources, attach source links, and mark uncertain details. A useful research record might have organization, role, public signal, potential relevance, source URL, checked date, and uncertainty. A personalized message should use one real detail that connects to the reason for writing. It should not be a paragraph of scraped facts designed to simulate a friendship.

### Step 3: Draft a short and honest message

A good first message explains who you are, why you chose this person, what small request you are making, and how they can decline. Keep it brief. Do not claim that you admire work you have not read or say that the product is used by companies that have not approved the claim. Avoid fake urgency, misleading subject lines, and attachments the recipient did not request.

Example:

> Subject: Quick question about client approvals  
> Hi Maya, I saw that your studio works with independent retailers on seasonal campaigns. I am exploring a simpler way to collect client sign-off on creative revisions. Is this a workflow you handle, or is there someone better to ask? No worries if it is not relevant.  
> Thanks,  
> Alex

This message does not pretend a solution is finished or ask for a purchase immediately. If you have a working product and a credible reason to offer a short trial, say so plainly. A genuine relationship is more valuable than theatrical personalization.

### Step 4: Review before sending

Use a human approval step before the first outreach, and keep it even after you have a repeatable process. Check the recipient, source, factual claims, tone, links, and intended action. Verify that the message follows applicable laws, the email provider's policies, and platform rules in the recipient's region. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and message type. Do not treat an AI-generated draft as legal advice.

If using Gmail or another connected service, test with your own address or a trusted internal recipient first. Confirm what the integration will do, what account it uses, and whether sending is immediate. Use the narrowest permissions available. Keep secrets out of prompts, documents, logs, and source control. Never assume an agent will stop safely if a task description is ambiguous.

### Step 5: Follow up respectfully

A single appropriate follow-up can be useful, but repeated messages after silence can become harassment. Decide in advance how many attempts are acceptable, how much time to leave, and when to stop. Any opt-out or request to stop should be honored immediately and recorded in a suppression list so the person is not re-added later.

Record minimal operational data: contact source, date, message version, response category, and next action. Avoid storing sensitive personal details or complete email threads unless there is a legitimate need and appropriate protection. Limit access and define when stale data will be deleted. A “do not contact” record may need to be retained to respect an opt-out, but it should contain only what is necessary.

## Measure the conversations that matter

Open rates are imperfect because privacy features and image loading can distort them. Prefer measures tied to actual learning: positive replies, useful interviews completed, qualified pilot interest, referrals, or a clear reason for rejection. Track the denominator. “Three replies” means little without knowing whether you sent five relevant notes or 500 generic ones.

Use a simple table for segment, source, date, message theme, status, response, learning, and follow-up. Avoid a complex CRM until necessary. After each batch, review whether the audience recognized the problem, the ask was manageable, and the message relied on a weak assumption. Change one major variable at a time.

## Create content from real material

Content works best when it answers audience questions, shows a product workflow, or shares a useful lesson. Sources include product updates, user questions, research, and founder experience. Do not invent stories, statistics, testimonials, or capabilities. Ask permission before using a customer's name, logo, quotation, or identifiable story. Anonymized examples are not automatically verified case studies.

Make a small content matrix:

| Audience question | Useful answer | Evidence or asset | Format | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How do I collect approvals? | A practical checklist | Your own workflow example | Blog post | Try the checklist |
| What changed this week? | Explain one improvement | Verified release note | Short social post | View the update |
| Is this tool for my team? | Show the intended use and limits | Product demo | Short video | Join a pilot |

Ask Friday to turn one source into several formats, but review every version for factual consistency. A blog post, social thread, product description, and short caption can all be derived from the same verified update, yet they should fit their audiences rather than repeat the same paragraph unchanged. Add a clear next step that is useful and proportional: read the guide, reply with a question, or try a demo.

### A reliable writing workflow

Choose an audience and question, gather approved source notes, and ask Friday for an outline. Check every number, quote, comparison, and product claim against the source. Edit into your natural voice, add descriptive headings and accessible image descriptions, and review permissions and links. Publish manually at first, then measure whether it helped the intended audience.

Content need not be long: a setup guide that answers a real question can outperform thin weekly posts. Bound your claims. Call beta features beta, and describe a method rather than inventing an untested result.

## Images and short-form creative

Images and clips should clarify a workflow. Use a redacted interface, labeled diagram, or simple illustration. Inspect generated text, accessibility, and accidental resemblance to real people or protected brands. Use assets you own or are licensed to use. Do not present generated images as real customers or evidence. Add informative alt text. For screenshots, use synthetic demo data and remove secrets and names.

## Scheduled content and follow-up systems

Recurring agents can remind you to review a draft, prepare a digest, or turn a verified changelog into a proposal. Start with a reviewed draft queue, not unattended publishing or bulk email. Set the schedule and time zone, owner, source links, review deadline, run limit, duplicate check, failure notification, and pause path. Test privately and record whether a human approved each output. Keep outreach draft-only until recipient selection, opt-outs, and sending behavior are verified.

Slack or another notification channel can report “three drafts are ready” or “the source page changed.” Keep alerts short and do not post recipient data, private messages, or secrets into a broad channel. Confirm channel membership and retention settings. Notifications should help you act, not become another source of noise.

## Three useful growth systems

### Founder Outreach Engine

Gather a small set of relevant prospects, record the public reason for fit, and draft personal notes for a review queue. A founder verifies each detail and sends manually or approves a tested integration. Report replies and learning, not just volume.

### Product update to content

Use an approved changelog to suggest a blog outline and social assets, with the source attached. Exclude unreleased roadmap details and security-sensitive changes. Publish only after checking that the feature is live and instructions work.

### Waitlist nurture

Send relevant follow-ups only to people who knowingly signed up. Collect minimal information, explain what subscribers receive, and make unsubscribing straightforward. Do not add scraped addresses or change the purpose of consent. Test links, timing, opt-outs, and a human reply route.

## Practical exercise: a two-week founder growth test

Pick one product idea and one audience you can reach appropriately. Your aim is learning, not scale.

1. Write a product promise and list evidence for its claims. Define one goal, such as five discovery conversations.
2. Identify 10 to 20 relevant contacts from appropriate sources. Record why each fits and where you found them.
3. Ask Friday to research only public professional context, with source links and unknowns marked.
4. Draft a short message, review each recipient and claim, then send a small batch through an appropriate channel.
5. Publish one useful piece based on a verified question or product fact, and invite focused feedback.
6. Record replies, opt-outs, and lessons without unnecessary personal data. After two weeks, state whether evidence supports changing the audience, message, product, or channel.

**Copy/paste prompt**

> Help me prepare a small, respectful user-research outreach test for [product] and [audience]. My goal is [learning goal]. Using only the public professional context and source links I provide, suggest why each person may be relevant, then draft a short message that accurately introduces me, makes one easy-to-decline request, and does not imply a relationship or unverified result. Return drafts for human review only. Do not find or scrape personal contact details, send messages, schedule follow-ups, or contact anyone. Mark uncertain facts and include an opt-out reminder in the workflow.

An exercise is successful even if few people respond, provided you learn why and adjust. A polite “not relevant” can be more informative than a vague signup.

## Common mistakes and practical checks

Before sending, publishing, or scheduling, ask:

- Is this person or audience genuinely relevant?
- Can I support every factual claim?
- Is the request easy to understand and easy to decline?
- Did I obtain permission for quotes, logos, images, or recordings?
- Am I following local rules and the provider's policies?
- Is there a clear opt-out, pause, or correction path?
- Could this workflow expose private data or credentials?
- Am I measuring qualified outcomes and learning rather than raw volume?

Avoid intrusive personalization, identical mass messages, false familiarity, unchecked AI drafts, and more scheduled content than you can maintain. Do not confuse views and likes with demand: they do not prove a person has the problem or intends to use your product.

## Cost, quality, and deliverability

Research, writing, image generation, and connected services may use credits, quotas, or paid plans. Costs vary with provider, model, and volume. Start small, set limits where available, and review usage before scaling. Never paste API keys into a prompt or public repository; use secure configuration.

Email deliverability depends on reputation, authentication, recipient relevance, list quality, and provider policies. It cannot be guaranteed by clever copy. Do not buy lists or attempt to bypass spam protections. Send only appropriate, expected messages, identify yourself honestly, and honor opt-outs. Test your domain configuration and sending setup using current provider guidance before relying on it for important communication.

Friday can make the process faster, but not make an irrelevant offer valuable or an unverified claim true. Keep the system small, human, measurable, and respectful. A few conversations and useful content beat an untrusted growth machine.

## Quick-start checklist

### What you are building

- Build a simple growth system for research, outreach, and content that helps the right people notice your product.
- Begin with a precise audience, a useful offer, and a realistic next step.
- Use respectful research and short, honest outreach before scaling anything.
- Measure learning and qualified replies, not vanity metrics alone.

### Your workflow in 6 easy steps

1. Choose one audience and one promise that is specific enough to test.
2. Identify a small list of justified contacts and record why they fit.
3. Research public context and draft a short, easy-to-decline message.
4. Review the message for factual clarity, trust, and tone before sending.
5. Track replies, refusals, and useful learnings in a simple system.
6. Publish one verified piece of content or update and use the response to improve the next message.
